1. Overview & Methodology

MaintEdge computes the scheduled Direct Maintenance Cost (DMC) for the Dornier 328 family of regional aircraft. The output is expressed in EUR or USD per Flight Hour (EUR/FH or USD/FH), the industry-standard metric for comparing aircraft maintenance economics.

The methodology follows a dual-interval occurrence model: each maintenance task has up to two intervals — one calendar-based (days or months) and one usage-based (flight hours, flight cycles, or APU hours). The limiting interval (whichever triggers first) determines the actual maintenance frequency.

3. Utilisation Profile

The utilisation profile defines how intensively the aircraft is operated annually. These parameters are the primary drivers for usage-based maintenance intervals.

Why it matters: The FH/FC ratio determines which interval — calendar or usage — is the limiting factor for each maintenance task. A low ratio (many short flights) means more cycles per hour, accelerating fatigue-driven inspections. A high ratio (fewer, longer flights) shifts the cost burden toward FH-driven tasks. The ratio also determines the PWC FMP engine rate tier for the D328eco.

3.2 APU Hours

The Do 328-100 and Do 328-300 (Jet) are equipped with an Auxiliary Power Unit (APU) for ground power and engine starting. APU operating hours drive the APU inspection intervals (APU500, APU800, APU1000, APU5000) and APU overhaul.

D328eco: The D328eco does not have an APU fitted. APU hours are set to zero and APU-related maintenance tasks are excluded from the calculation.

4. Engine Program (FMP vs. No FMP)

The D328eco offers two engine maintenance costing options. This selection fundamentally changes how engine DMC is computed.

FMP Fleet Management Program

  • Pay-per-hour model via Pratt & Whitney Canada
  • Rate determined by average flight duration
  • Covers: overhaul, shop labour & parts, HSI, LLP replacement (incl. HPT Blades), engine BUR, SB cat 1–6, DPHM/ECTM, FAST connectivity, fuel nozzle refurbishment — LLP HPT Blades are included in the FMP rate and are not a separate DMC line item
  • Propeller overhaul is a separate line item
  • PWC-calibrated environment factor applied — benign baseline ×1.00; single adverse environment up to +25%

No FMP Traditional Overhaul

  • Hard Time / Fixed Interval Overhaul — Engine: 8,000 FH TBO
  • Hard Time / Fixed Interval Overhaul — Turbomachine: 8,000 FH TBO
  • Hard Time / Fixed Interval Overhaul — RGB: 8,000 FH TBO
  • Hot Section Inspection — 4,000 FH
  • HPT Blades — 15,000 FC
  • More conservative — higher exposure to cost variability

4.1 PWC FMP Environment Adjustment

Disclaimer — Environmental & Operational Adjustment Factors:

The environment and operational adjustment factors applied to airframe, propeller, and landing gear systems (Sections 5–6) are derived from engineering data sources including D328 Legacy aircraft reliability reports, defect and finding reports, structural fatigue analyses, and regional turboprop fleet maintenance benchmarks. Factor weights are calibrated against Dornier 328 Maintenance Planning Document (MPD) data, operator reliability feedback, and recognised industry-standard maintenance cost methodologies for commuter and regional turboprop aircraft of comparable design and operational profile. These factors represent engineering estimates and should be validated against operator-specific fleet experience before use in contractual or financial planning.

FMP-specific environment factors (this section) are derived separately from Pratt & Whitney Canada (P&WC) rate data and benchmarks for the PW127XT-S engine under the Fleet Management Program, and are not interchangeable with the general airframe/systems weights described above.
EnvironmentFMP FactorMax IncreasePWC Environment TypeEngineering Rationale
Temperate ×1.00 +0% Benign baseline (PWC explicit) No adverse contaminants. Rates quoted at this level by default.
High Altitude ×1.25 +25% Volcanic ash Volcanic ash is extremely abrasive and harder than engine blade materials. Rapid compressor and turbine erosion. Worst-case single environment — set at the PWC stated maximum.
Arid / Desert ×1.22 +22% Sand Sand ingestion erodes compressor and turbine blades, clogs fuel nozzles, and degrades seal faces. TBO reduction is well-documented for desert operations.
Coastal / Marine ×1.20 +20% Salt Salt ingestion causes Type I and Type II hot corrosion on HPT/LPT blades and vanes. Sulfidation accelerates hot-section degradation and reduces time between shop visits.
Tropical / Humid ×1.13 +13% Industrial air pollution Sulfur compounds, heavy metals, and organic chemicals in industrial air cause hot corrosion and combustor fouling. Tropical humidity accelerates deposit formation.
Cold / Arctic ×1.08 +8% Cold operations Repeated cold-soaked starts increase wear on turbine seals and oil systems. High thermal cycling from cold ambient to operating temperature stresses hot-section components.
Combined environments: When multiple adverse environments are selected (e.g., Coastal + Arid), the blended factor naturally exceeds any single-environment value. For extreme multi-environment cases, PWC provides rates on a case-by-case basis — treat calculator output as an estimate in such scenarios.

5. Operating Environment Mix

Operational environments affect aircraft maintenance costs through mechanisms such as corrosion, thermal cycling, sand/dust ingestion, and material degradation. MaintEdge models this through a blended environment system with category-specific weights, reflecting that different environments impact different aircraft systems to varying degrees.

5.1 Base Environment Factors

Each environment has a base severity multiplier representing the overall cost impact relative to temperate baseline:

EnvironmentBase FactorBlendingRationale
Temperate x1.00 Blended (100% total) Baseline condition — moderate temperature, humidity, and altitude. Western/Central European climate reference.
Tropical / Humid x1.12 Blended High humidity accelerates corrosion, fungal growth, and rubber degradation. Intense UV exposure degrades composites and sealants. Higher thermal loads on avionics cooling systems.
Arid / Desert x1.08 Blended Sand and dust ingestion erodes compressor blades and clogs filters. Extreme diurnal thermal cycling stresses seals and bonded joints. Low humidity reduces corrosion but increases static discharge risk.
Coastal / Marine x1.10 Blended Salt-laden atmosphere is the primary corrosion accelerator. Aggressive attack on aluminium alloys, steel fasteners, and landing gear. Increased galvanic corrosion at dissimilar metal joints.
Cold / Arctic x1.06 Blended Thermal contraction stresses seals and fuel system components. De-icing chemical exposure degrades paint and sealants. Cold-soaking increases engine start wear and hydraulic system strain.
High Altitude x1.04 Independent adder Airfields above 5,000 ft elevation. Higher engine power settings for takeoff reduce engine life margins. Reduced air density affects propeller loads. Applied independently, not blended.

A weight of 1.0 means the full base factor applies; 0.3 means only 30% of the incremental effect is applied; 1.5 means an amplified 150% effect.

Rationale for Key Weight Assignments

Weight PatternEngineering Rationale
Tropical → Corrosion/SSI high (1.5) Humidity is the primary corrosion driver. Tropical environments combine high humidity with temperature, creating an aggressive electrochemical corrosion cycle. Structural sampling is directly affected as it detects corrosion-related degradation.
Tropical → Engines low (0.5) Tropical air is warm, dense, and clean (no sand/dust). Engine compressor erosion is minimal. Thermal cycling is moderate due to consistent ambient temperatures.
Arid → Propellers very high (1.5) Sand and fine particulate cause severe leading-edge erosion on propeller blades. Desert operations can halve propeller blade life due to FOD and abrasion.
Arid → Corrosion very low (0.2) Dry environments suppress electrochemical corrosion. Minimal moisture means corrosion prevention tasks are rarely limiting.
Coastal → Landing Gear high (1.3) Salt spray attacks steel and chrome components. Landing gear is directly exposed to salt-laden runway spray during takeoff and landing at coastal airfields.
Cold → APU high (1.2) Cold-soaking increases APU start cycles and operating time. Extended ground warm-up periods stress APU turbine components. De-icing fluid contamination adds to APU intake filter loading.
High Altitude → Engines very high (1.5) Reduced air density at elevation requires higher engine power settings for takeoff, increasing turbine temperature and reducing hot-section life margins. This is the dominant effect of high-altitude operations.

5.3 Blending Logic

The five operational environments (Temperate, Tropical, Arid, Coastal, Cold) are blended as a weighted average summing to 100%. This models mixed-fleet operations where an aircraft operates across multiple climate zones.

-- Blended environment factor per category
for each category:
  blended_factor = Σ (weight_fractionenv × [1.0 + (baseenv - 1.0) × cat_weightenv])

-- Example: 60% Temperate + 40% Coastal on Landing Gear
  Temperate: 1.0 + (1.00 - 1.0) × 1.0 = 1.000
  Coastal: 1.0 + (1.10 - 1.0) × 1.3 = 1.130
  Blended = 0.60 × 1.000 + 0.40 × 1.130 = 1.052

5.4 High Altitude Operations

High Altitude is treated as an independent adder, not blended with the five operational environments. This reflects that an aircraft can operate from high-altitude airfields regardless of its climate zone. The user sets a percentage (0–100%) representing the proportion of operations from airfields above 5,000 ft.

-- High Altitude adder (applied on top of blended environment)
HA_addercategory = (1.04 - 1.0) × HA_weightcategory × (HA_pct / 100)
final_env_factor = blended_factor + HA_adder

6. Operational Condition Factors

Beyond climate, two operational profiles introduce additional maintenance stress: gravel runway operations and STOL (Short Takeoff and Landing) operations. Both are expressed as a percentage of total operations.

6.1 Gravel Runway Operations

Operating from unpaved or gravel surfaces exposes the aircraft to Foreign Object Damage (FOD), stone chips, and abrasive particulate ingestion. The base severity is +15% at 100% gravel operations, applied with category-specific weights.

-- Gravel factor per category
gravel_base = (gravel_pct / 100) × 0.15
gravel_factorcategory = 1.0 + gravel_base × weightcategory
CategoryWeightRationale
Propellers1.5Direct stone strike damage on blades; leading-edge erosion is the primary life-limiting factor on gravel strips.
Landing Gear1.4Wheel assemblies, brakes, and tyres absorb the highest FOD impact. Gravel impingement accelerates tyre wear and damages brake components.
Airframe Checks0.6Belly skin and lower fuselage panels suffer from stone chips and abrasion, but protected areas are less affected.
Engines0.6Inlet FOD and particle ingestion erode compressor blades. Gravel guards mitigate but do not eliminate the risk.
Corrosion Prevention0.5Paint damage from stone chips exposes bare metal to corrosion. Secondary effect of physical damage.
Structural Sampling0.4Moderate increase in inspection scope for belly structure and lower panels in gravel-ops fleet.
FH-Based Tasks0.4Minor increase in inspection frequency for engine-related FH tasks due to particulate exposure.
Time Controlled Components0.4Some TCCs (filters, screens) have accelerated replacement in dusty/gravel environments.
Fatigue Damage0.3Runway roughness increases ground loads, with minor fatigue implications for lower fuselage structure.
APU Inspections0.2Minimal effect -- APU inlet is positioned away from primary FOD zones.
APU0.2Minimal effect on APU overhaul intervals.

6.2 STOL Operations

Short Takeoff and Landing operations impose higher aerodynamic and structural loads due to steeper approach angles, higher touchdown rates, and aggressive braking. The base severity is +10% at 100% STOL operations.

-- STOL factor per category
stol_base = (stol_pct / 100) × 0.10
stol_factorcategory = 1.0 + stol_base × weightcategory
CategoryWeightRationale
Landing Gear1.5Hard landings and aggressive braking are the dominant STOL stressor. Higher sink rates increase gear fatigue loads and accelerate brake/tyre wear.
Fatigue Damage1.5STOL manoeuvres generate higher g-loads in both takeoff rotation and landing flare. Wing root and fuselage fatigue accumulation is significantly accelerated.
Propellers1.2Full-reverse thrust on short runways increases propeller blade loads and reverser mechanism wear.
Engines0.8Higher power settings for short-field takeoff increase turbine temperature exceedances. Reverse thrust adds engine cycle severity.
Airframe Checks0.5Increased flap and slat cycling, plus higher structural loads, add to airframe inspection scope.
FH-Based Tasks0.4Moderate increase in wear-related inspection tasks.
Structural Sampling0.3STOL loads are within the design envelope but closer to limit, increasing the importance of structural monitoring.
APU0.3Minor effect from increased ground operations time at short-field destinations.

Rationale

  • Engines +4% (MOD 20/30): The PW119C produces 5% more thermodynamic power than the PW119B, operating closer to its thermal limits. This slightly accelerates hot-section wear.
  • Propellers +2% (MOD 20/30): Higher engine output translates to increased propeller blade loading, marginally accelerating fatigue accumulation.

  • Landing Gear +1% (MOD 30): Higher maximum landing weight capability in MOD 30 increases gear cycle severity.

8. Labour Rate

The labour rate (EUR/hr) is a user-defined input representing the fully burdened cost of one maintenance man-hour, including direct wages, benefits, overhead, tooling, and facility costs.

RangeClassificationTypical Regions
EUR 20 – 49Low-cost regionSoutheast Asia, North Africa, parts of South America
EUR 50 – 99Mid-range (typical EASA/FAA MRO)Eastern Europe, Turkey, Middle East, Brazil
EUR 100 – 250PremiumWestern Europe, North America, Scandinavia, Japan
Note: The labour rate applies only to the labour component of DMC. Material costs are fixed OEM prices and are independent of labour rate. A higher labour rate increases the labour share of total DMC but does not affect material-driven categories like engine overhaul.
-- Labour DMC per item
DMC_labour = (Occurrence × Labour_Rate × Man_Hours) / FH_per_year

9. Combined Factor Computation

All adjustment factors are combined multiplicatively per category. This reflects the compounding nature of operational stresses — an aircraft operating on gravel runways in a tropical environment faces both effects simultaneously.

-- Combined adjustment factor per category
Combined = Env_factor × Gravel_factor × STOL_factor × MOD_factor

-- Where each sub-factor is category-specific:
Env_factor = blended_env(category) + HA_adder(category)
Gravel_factor = 1.0 + (gravel_pct/100 × 0.15) × gravel_weight(category)
STOL_factor = 1.0 + (stol_pct/100 × 0.10) × stol_weight(category)
MOD_factor = MOD_FACTORS[variant][category]

Example: Full Factor Calculation

Scenario: Do 328-100 MOD 30, 70% Temperate + 30% Coastal, 20% Gravel, 15% STOL, 10% High Altitude

Landing Gear category:
• Env blended: 0.70 × 1.000 + 0.30 × [1.0 + 0.10 × 1.3] = 0.70 × 1.000 + 0.30 × 1.130 = 1.039
• HA adder: 0.04 × 0.4 × (10/100) = 0.0016 → Env total = 1.0406
• Gravel: 1.0 + (0.20 × 0.15) × 1.4 = 1.042
• STOL: 1.0 + (0.15 × 0.10) × 1.5 = 1.0225
• MOD 30: 1.01
• Combined = 1.0406 × 1.042 × 1.0225 × 1.01 = 1.1192 (+11.9% over baseline)

10. DMC Calculation Engine

For each maintenance item, the engine computes DMC in EUR/FH through the following steps:

-- Step 1: Calendar-based occurrence
if interval is Days:   Occ_calendar = 365 / interval_days
if interval is Months: Occ_calendar = 12 / interval_months

-- Step 2: Usage-based occurrence
if param = FH:    Occ_usage = FH_per_year / interval
if param = FC:    Occ_usage = FC_per_year / interval
if param = APU Hrs: Occ_usage = APU_hrs_per_year / interval

-- Step 3: Limiting interval (whichever triggers more frequently)
Occ_used = max(Occ_calendar, Occ_usage)

-- Step 4: Base DMC
DMC_labour   = (Occ_used × Labour_Rate × Man_Hours) / FH_per_year
DMC_material = (Occ_used × Material_Cost) / FH_per_year

-- Step 5: Apply adjustment factors
DMC_labour_adj   = DMC_labour × Combined_Factor
DMC_material_adj = DMC_material × Combined_Factor

-- Step 6: Total
DMC_total = DMC_labour_adj + DMC_material_adj (EUR/FH)
FMP exception: D328eco with FMP enabled does not compute engine DMC through the occurrence model. Instead, the PWC FMP base rate (interpolated from the $2026 table) is multiplied by the PWC FMP environment factor (see §4.1) and injected as a material cost in EUR/FH. The FMP rate already covers LLP replacement (incl. HPT Blades) — no separate LLP line is added.

11. All-In DMC Estimate

Beyond scheduled maintenance, operators face additional costs that MaintEdge estimates using industry benchmarks for regional turboprop aircraft:

Component% of Scheduled DMCBasis
Scheduled DMC 100% Computed by MaintEdge (all maintenance items)
Unscheduled Maintenance +40% Industry benchmark for defect rectification, AOG events, and unplanned component replacements. Based on IATA MCC and regional turboprop fleet data.
Logistics & Customs +15% Shipping, customs clearance, warehousing, and procurement overhead. Particularly significant for operators in remote regions or island networks.
All-In Estimate = 155% Total estimated cost including all components above.
-- All-in estimate
Unscheduled   = Scheduled_DMC × 0.40
Logistics    = Scheduled_DMC × 0.15
All_In_DMC = Scheduled_DMC + Unscheduled + Logistics = Scheduled_DMC × 1.55
Important: These are industry benchmarks and should be validated against operator-specific experience. Actual unscheduled maintenance costs vary significantly based on fleet age, MRO capability, spares strategy, and operational discipline. Users should apply professional judgement for contractual or financial planning.
